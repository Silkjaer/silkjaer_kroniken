He married five days after his father’s death and three days before his funeral. It sounds like a scandal, and it was not.
@01c36f0e5ed9
The marriage entry itself settles it. In the column for witnesses there are two names: N. Nielsen Skytte and <t1>Thommas Jensen</t1> — the groom’s father. The witnesses’ declaration was made when the banns were published, and under the ordinance of 1824 the pastor had to read the banns from the pulpit on three Sundays in a row; for a wedding on 14 November that means from the middle of October. So the father had signed while he was alive: the wedding was being prepared before he drowned, and was neither arranged nor hurried by his death. It was the custom of the house: when Thomas himself stood as a bridegroom in 1853, the witnesses were the two fathers — his own and the bride’s (chapter 3). And the last column of the entry says “In the church” — not a quiet wedding in a home, but a public one. Five days after the sea took the father, the son stood at the altar, and the father’s name stood on the paper. [S28]

1878 was also the year another register closed around him. The levying roll for Husby — district 28 — had known him since 1873, when he was entered at eighteen: <t1>Niels Thomsen</t1>, son of smallholder Thomas Jensen, born in Husby on 9 July 1855, 62¾ inches tall — about 164 centimetres. In 1878 the draft board added its note: 29½ inches round the chest, found exempt from military service — and the entry was struck through diagonally, as was done when a man was deleted from the roll. The army had no use for the slim fisherman from Økjær. The sea made other demands: from January 1879 he stood by the lifeboat at Vedersø, and there he stayed for thirty-seven years. [S113]

@aa04686b5f50
The deeds tie the ends together: the father, Thomas Jensen, bought the house on 13b in 1854/55, and in January 1879 — two months after the wreck — Niels acquired title to the place by allotment in his estate. So Niels did not move to a strange farm; he took over his father’s house in the marsh, and the name followed — first with the children, then with himself. The history of the property from the parcelling-off in 1851 is told in chapter 1; the documentation — and the line of owners up to the sale out of the family in 1924 — is in <t1>Appendix A</t1>. The middle step of the staircase can be dated. In the census of 1890 the household stands on matr. 13b with the parents recorded as <t1>Niels Thomsen</t1> and <t2>Ane Jensen</t2> — and with all five children recorded as Silkjær: Ane Marie Nielsen Silkjær, Thomas Nielsen Silkjær, Ane Johanne Nielsen Silkjær, Jens Klaus Nielsen Silkjær and Esper Nielsen Silkjær. The father Thomas had already appeared as Thomas Jensen Silkjær in a deed of 1876, and the newspapers used the name in 1878 — but in Niels’ own household it came in from below. [S60, S64]
@54a090f0e7ae
So the name entered the papers from below, through the children, while the parents still stood under the patronymic. And it applied to the whole family of children: Ane Marie too, the daughter of the first wife, was given the farm name. Silkjær clung to the household and the place, not to one particular mother’s children. The last step came with a document: on 21 November 1905 Niels received a certificate permitting him to bear the name Niels Silkjær — the farm name had become a legal family name. The parish register carried the certificate all the way back to the starting point: in the margin by his baptismal entry of 1855 the name Silkjær was added, “according to certificate”.<t1>?</t1><n1/> [S64, S65]
