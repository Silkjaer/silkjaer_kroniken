The record seems to write “Høsten 1911” (the harvest of 1911) for when it began, where the judgment and Thomas’ statement have “Sommeren 1911” (the summer of 1911) — the two are compatible if the beginning lay in the haymaking of late summer, and the chronicle follows the judgment’s wording in the main text. Anders Hejde’s son is unnamed in the record — it gives only his age. Pastor Hornbech
@13c91b4dec71
The travelling preacher to whom Kristine Oline wrote is entered in the police record book as “Pastor Hornbeck fra Randers, der som Rejsepræst for Indre Mission flere Gange har besøgt Klitten i de senere Aar” (Pastor Hornbeck of Randers, who as travelling preacher for the Inner Mission has several times visited the dunes in recent years). He is identified with high probability as Carl Marius Hornbech (1871—1934): matriculated from Randers Latin School in 1890, cand.theol. 1896, missionary in India 1902—09 — where his first wife Olga died in 1908 — then travelling preacher from 1909, living in Randers, and from 1915 parish priest at St Michael’s Church in Fredericia, where he died in 1934. That a Pastor Hornbech lived in Randers in exactly the years of the case is independently confirmed by the town’s school programmes for 1912—14, which list a child with the father given as “Hornbech (Præst)”. One discrepancy has since been cleared up: the police record book calls him travelling preacher for the Inner Mission, but the police report in the criminal file — written three weeks earlier, from the family’s own information — lists him as “en Pastor Hornbek, der bor i Randers, men som er Rejsepræst for dansk Missionsselskab” (a Pastor Hornbek who lives in Randers but is travelling preacher for the Danish Missionary Society). That is precisely Carl Marius Hornbech’s society; the record book’s “Inner Mission” must then be counted as the clerk’s imprecision, and the identification is strengthened accordingly. The report also confirms the letters: the priest had told Anders Hejde of Bjerregaard that he had “modtaget Breve fra den besvangrede om Forholdet” (received letters from the pregnant girl about the matter), and that she had written to him because he had held a service on the dunes, “og at hun ikke havde syntes, at hun kunde betro sig til nogen her hjemme” (and that she had not felt she could confide in anyone here at home). The name is spelt three ways in the material: the record book’s “Hornbeck”, the report’s “Hornbek” and the man’s own form “Hornbech”. The chronicle uses Hornbech in its own text, while quotations and transcriptions keep the sources’ spellings — that also applies to the label “the Hornbeck passage”, which names the record book’s own wording. No source otherwise connects him directly with the case. His letter to her has not been found, and it is not known whether it was ever handed in to the authorities; the scene with the letter stands as Kristiane told it in court on 7 March. The police also corresponded with the parish priest on Holmsland Klit on 8—10 March; the content is unknown. [S120, S124, S174, S176]

The pardon case and the date of the resolution
@9c490b43ef91
The chain in the pardon case is documented in the archives of the county and the prison: the defence counsel, solicitor Kamp, submitted the petition; the judge recommended it in a statement of 7 July 1913; the county forwarded it on 8 July to the Ministry of Justice through the Director of Prisons “idet bem., at Amtet kan henholde sig til den af Pol. afgivne Erklæring” (noting that the county can refer to the statement given by the police); the ministry refused on 17 July, and the refusal was sent on to the institution and entered in its journal. On 10 November Vridsløselille recommended seven prisoners for pardon on the occasion of the fiftieth anniversary of the House of Glücksburg’s accession to the throne, and the royal resolution of 15 November pardoned all seven; the three foreign prisoners with expulsion from the realm. The prison register’s endorsement of release “kgl. Res. s.D.” (royal resolution, same day) and the class record’s “Ben. kgl. Res. 15/11” (pardoned, royal resolution 15/11) both date the resolution to 15 November. In the prison’s letter the word “med” in “dog med Henvisning til Dommerens Anbefaling” (though with reference to the judge’s recommendation) is read with slight uncertainty, and the journal’s note that the refusal was communicated to the prisoner is a likely reading of an abbreviated endorsement. The letter’s count of children — “3 ægte Børn, 1 uægte Barn, 1 Blodskamsbarn og 3 Stedbørn” (3 legitimate children, 1 illegitimate child, 1 child of incest and 3 stepchildren) — does not tally with the known children: Thomas and Kristiane had three children together (Kristen b. 1904 before the marriage, Anna b. 1907 and Niels b. 1910), so either Kristen is counted as the illegitimate child and the number of legitimate children is a mistake, or he was counted as legitimate at the marriage and the illegitimate child is another, unknown to the sources; Else Margrethe had not yet been born on 10 November. The question has not been settled. Finally, the county’s day journal records an exchange with the district office on 26 September—4 October 1913, whose content is not apparent from the journal.
