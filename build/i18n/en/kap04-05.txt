The register of deaths has a column for the cause of death. In 1863 it is empty — for Anne Marie, for Kirstine and for several others in the parish. The silence is not the household’s; it is the year’s. In 1869, by contrast, the column is in use, and one word recurs: <t1>halssyge</t1>, throat sickness. The pastor wrote it beside a two-year-old, a four-and-a-half-year-old, two adults of thirty and the neighbours’ child whose entry stands just before Karen Marie’s. @2a99e16bbda3
Halssyge was the nineteenth-century word for a throat infection with a danger of choking. It most often covered diphtheria, but was also used for croup and for scarlet fever affecting the throat; the pastor wrote down what could be seen, not a medical diagnosis. The chapter therefore gives the source’s word and puts no disease on any of the three dead. The neighbours’ child’s entry says something about what was going round the parish — not what Karen Marie died of. [S38] <t1>All caveats →</t1>

@1bf86690b968
The parish register’s wording, verbatim: “<t1>drowned when capsizing with a sea-boat</t1>” (druknet ved kæntring med havskib). A havskib was the flat-bottomed West Jutland beach boat, launched straight from the open beach — there was no harbour. The accident happened on a Saturday. Before the week was out, the whole event was in print in at least two newspapers: a letter written from the west coast that same day was printed in <t2>Dannevirke in Haderslev on 13 November</t2> and in <t3>Korsør Avis on 14 November 1878</t3>. The account was written on the day of the accident, and Thomas Jensen is named in it.<t4>?</t4><n1/>
@7af7b7f568ff
The newspapers’ date, place and names agree with the parish register; both papers list “beach bailiff Thomas Jensen Silkjær” among the dead. The newspapers, written on the day of the accident, reported that none of the bodies had drifted ashore; the parish register’s collective note at the burials eight days later singles out only one body that did not come ashore — and it was not his. The aftermath
@3f93473622e3
The survivor walked home. Esper Jensen was badly shaken when he came ashore, but after resting a little while at the Stage lifeboat station, changing into dry clothes and taking some refreshment, he walked to his home, which lay a good way from the station. The letter-writer calls it a trait typical of the West Jutland fisherman’s toughness.
@95b14887c5fc
And then the newspaper draws up the account of those left behind. The last three named — Anders Knudsen, Laurids Mikkelsen and Thomas Jensen Silkjær — were reasonably well off, with grown-up children; only Anders Knudsen’s one child was 11. It was worst for the two smallholding families from Kløve: Peder Jensen left seven children not yet confirmed, the youngest not three months old, and Iver Kristian Olesen four children, the eldest not yet eight. Both families were extremely poor. Only by the fathers’ “untiring diligence and ability” had they earned their daily bread without becoming a burden to anyone, and now they would have to turn to poor relief unless a collection were started.

Which station
@1922c7f38e67
The newspaper calls it “the Stage lifeboat station”, and that is not the station’s official name. The lifeboat service’s own book of 1927 knows only one station here: <t1>Vedersø</t1>, founded in 1852. Its first superintendent was beach bailiff Chr. M. From land the accident was seen, and help was given at once, which was the more easily done as the accident happened close to the Stage lifeboat station, and both the lifeboat and the rocket apparatus were immediately brought to the scene of the accident, but the sea had risen into such violent uproar — it was, namely, heavy squally weather — that the lifeboat could not go out, and although 2 rockets were fired, no rescue was possible, since he for whom the rockets were chiefly meant, farmer Anders Knudsen, who swam about for a long time on an oar after the others had drowned, had either become so exhausted that he could not grasp the line thrown to him by the rocket apparatus, or did not see it; he made no attempt to grasp the line, and at last he sank.
