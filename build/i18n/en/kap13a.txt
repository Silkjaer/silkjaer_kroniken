The first document in which she stands as the one making a claim is older than you might think — it came three days after the baptism. On 18 June 1913 the district bailiff’s office opened a new case in the list of cases: <t1>“Pat. S. Christine Oline Enevoldsen v. Thomas Nielsen Silkjær”</t1> — a paternity case, number 82/1913. She stood as first party. Seventeen years old, a month after the birth, with the child’s father in Ringkjøbing jail, she stood as a party in a maintenance case against her stepfather — the same road her mother had taken in February. The case was closed as early as 20 June; the maintenance order itself lay with the county and has not been found. But the single line in the list of cases is enough to show that the boy’s support was put on a formal footing while his father was inside — and that the case stood in her name. [S121]
@4c05f1076645
That the payments also had to be enforced is shown by a find a year and a half later. <t1>Politiets Efterretninger</t1> — the same gazette that in February 1913 had carried the arrest — lists him in no. 12 of 28 January 1915 under the heading <t2>“Wanted maintenance debtors”</t2>: debtors the authorities were looking for because an ordered payment stood unpaid. Which payment was in arrears, the notice does not say — it may have been Arne’s or Else Margrethe’s, but also the older maintenance case of March 1913 concerning Kristiane and the three children of the marriage. But he had not vanished: he was living with his family in Esbjerg, where the census finds them together the year after, and <t3>the day after the notice, on 29 January 1915, Kristiane gave birth to his son Ejlerth Marius at Hjertinggade 13</t3>. While his name stood in print in the police gazette as a wanted maintenance debtor, his wife lay in childbed with the next child. The notice must therefore be read for what it proves — arrears and a gazette that did not know where the man was — not as a sign that he had left home again. [S76, S178]

The sources show the arrangement by February 1916 at the latest: the boy was then in Esbjerg, while his mother had only just come from Holmsland. When Arne was separated from her and came into his grandmother’s household, no known source says — nor who decided it, whether it was meant as a temporary arrangement, or what any of them thought about it. An unmarried mother of seventeen in 1913 seldom had that kind of choice on her own. Where on Holmsland she was in the years between remains open — and no census can answer: the only one between 1911 and 1921 is precisely the census of February 1916, and by then she was already in Esbjerg.
