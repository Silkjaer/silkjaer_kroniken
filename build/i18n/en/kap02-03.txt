For centuries drifting sand had eaten its way across the fields. The soil was poor, the wind blew from the west all year round, and in winter the storms could take roofs, boats and crops in a single night. And there lay the sea, a few kilometres from the church, without a single harbour.

One word in the line deserves to be read slowly. Ole Jensen Klit was a <t1>strandfoged</t1> — a beach bailiff. It was not an office you applied for; it was one you were appointed to. The beach bailiff was the authorities’ man on his stretch of coast: he had to keep watch over wrecks, salvage and record what the sea threw up — timber, rope, barrels, and the drowned — and answer for it to the wreck commissioner. On a coast without a harbour this was the post where the parish’s relationship with the sea turned into paperwork.
@1f91923c513e
Sixty-two years after his death his grandson drowned in the same water — with the same office on his shoulders. Thomas Jensen too was a beach bailiff, says the newspaper that reported his death.

Anne Marie Jansdatter
@20f03c566827
1827 — 1863 · the Tarpgaard family, Vedersø
@3844183530c1
When she stood as a bride in Vedersø Church on 20 November 1853, she was not marrying up. The marriage entry records the groom as “bachelor and fisherman Thomas Jensen of Husby Klit” — he had no roof of his own but lived with his brother Ole Jensen out on the dunes. The witnesses were the two fathers: Jan Andersen Tarpgaard and Jens Olesen. A fisherman from the dunes owned less land and more risk than the home she came from; what she thought of the exchange we do not know, for no one wrote it down. But she made it, and moved from the fields of Tarpgaard to the line of dunes by the sea. [S68]
@201cfd4c5cae
There the young couple stayed for a year or so. Then Thomas bought the house in the marsh — the deed was signed in December 1854 (chapter 1) — and they moved the few kilometres inland. Seen across the family’s long history, the shift from sand to marsh looks like a drift over generations; in the life actually lived it was a single year in a young marriage. And with a house of their own came a new title: the fisherman from the dunes appears in the following year’s papers as smallholder and fisherman.
@7e9f54cec31c
On 9 July 1855, three months after the deed was registered, she gave birth to their son Niels: the young couple gained a home and a son in the same spring.
@633ca42dbb94
The census of 1 February that year shows the house as it stood while she was carrying the child. Thomas, thirty, “smallholder and fisherman; lives mostly by fishing”. Anne Marie, twenty-eight. And two who stayed on after the sale: the seller Niels Jensen, fifty-three, now “retired man, kept by the householder” — and his unmarried stepdaughter, Sidsel Cathrine, thirty-five, who made her living by spinning. The young couple did not move into an empty house: four adults and a child on the way, out in the marsh. [S60] And when the boy was carried to baptism in July, both worlds turned out: among the godparents stood “Niels Poulsgaard” — by all appearances the retired man himself, the man who had sold them the house — and Johanne Cathrine, married to Thomas’ brother Esper, who had also moved in from the dunes to Øe. “The girl Ane Marie Jensdatter” and “bachelor Ole Jensen” may be Thomas’ sister and brother of the same names.
