@d6059c629705
Silkjær was a place long before it became a family name. The name appears as a locality in its own right in Husby parish in <t1>Danmarks Stednavne</t1>, the national register of place names — a field, a marsh, a property. How old it is, we do not know. But it was the name of the place and not of the family when the first of them we can follow were born out on Sønder Klit in the 1700s.
@7b5769eaac0b
Today the place lies at Øhusevej 1B in Vester Husby — cadastral no. 13b, divided off from the farm at Øe. The sources call it two things: the parish registers write Økjær, the censuses Øe. These are not two addresses. Øe was the cadastral district, the larger designation; Økjær was the parish’s name for this part of it — the marsh and the houses around it. A family could therefore live on 13b and still have Økjær written in the parish register.<t1>?</t1><n1/>
@a3dbd8a13463
The censuses of 1890, 1901 and 1911 record the household directly on matr. 13b, and the land register follows the title from the deed of 1854/55 to the sale out of the family in 1924. That Økjær covered several households can be seen with Thomas Jensen’s brother Esper: his children are born at “Øe”, the newspapers call him Ø., and at his death in 1883 the parish register records him as a smallholder “at Økjær” — the same household, three designations. The property that bears the name Økjær today lies on cadastral no. 15 and is a different property from the family’s. The line of owners is set out in <t1>Appendix A</t1>. [S60, S61, S65, S66]

The strongest local parallel points to “the willow marsh”: the farm Silbjerg in the same parish — it lies closer to Husby Sø, some way west of Silkjær — was explained as early as 1900 from <t1>silja</t1>, an older name for the willow — the slope “has in old times been overgrown with willow”. @54316a454528
The papers let us watch the property come into view. At Øe lay the farm on cadastral no. 13, which <t1>Thomas Miltersen</t1> had until then owned and lived on. In 1847 he handed the farm over to his son Jens Thomsen. In 1851 the son had a piece of the land parcelled off: parcel no. 2, cadastral no. 13b, out towards the low marsh. That same summer he sold the parcel to Niels Jensen Poulsgaard, who had himself been born at Øe and had just been widowed. [S40, S61, S62, S63]
@9080e3beac4d
Niels Jensen kept it only a few years. By a deed signed on 18 December 1854 and registered on 4 April 1855 he sold it on to a young smallholder and fisherman, <t1>Thomas Jensen of Husby</t1>. What changed hands the deed describes in a single phrase: “a detached house, situated in Silkjær” (»et fritliggende Huus, der er beliggende i Silkjær«). The deeds suggest that the house may have been built during his short ownership — the deed of 1851 mentions no buildings, while that of 1854 expressly refers to a house — but they cannot prove it. The seller stayed on in the house as a retired man in the new householder’s keeping. [S60, S63]

»beliggende i Silkjær« — “situated in Silkjær” — three words in the deed signed on 18 December 1854 and registered on 4 April 1855: the oldest dated source form of the name. Here Silkjær is used to describe a location: the house lay in the locality that bore the name.

@ccd2ab3d75c2
The three words are the name’s oldest dated attestation — and its most down to earth. Here Silkjær does not follow anyone’s name: it describes the place where the house stood, and the buyer is simply called Thomas Jensen. So he did not buy a house that was later named after the family. He bought a house, perhaps quite new, in a place that was already called Silkjær. <t1>The family came to the place first; the name came to the family afterwards.</t1>
@c75e6a6af119
This is how place names could become personal names in the district. They told where a person belonged. And because a small parish held far too many people called Niels, Jens and Thomas, the place also became a way of telling people apart.

@7ec032450956
Danmarks Stednavne records Silkjær as a locality in its own right in Husby parish, Ulfborg Hundred. The oldest dated record found is the deed of 1854/55 [S63]. The second element <t1>kær</t1> is common throughout the West Jutland lowland and consistently denotes wet, low-lying ground.
@e5114a1910df
The first element has a close parallel in the farm name <t1>Silbjerg</t1> in the same parish — Storgaard Pedersen places the farm south-east of the rectory, and the local archive describes it as lying closer to Husby Sø, some way west of Silkjær. Silbjerg and Silkjær are two different farms and must not be confused. [S115] P. Storgaard Pedersen wrote in 1900 that Silbjerg “probably took its name from the slope having in old times been overgrown with willow, whose older name was Silja” — and <t2>selje/silje</t2>, in Norse form <t3>silja</t3>, is given in the dictionaries as an older name for kinds of willow, known also in Danish and Jutlandic usage. If the two names share a first element, Silkjær is the marsh with siljer: the willow marsh. Trap Danmark also knows another Silbjerg in Staby parish, written “Silbiers Bol” in 1688 — the two farms must be kept apart in the sources. The oldest spelling found — “Silkjær” in the deed of 1854/55 — looks like the modern one and does not settle the question; what would settle it is an even older form such as <t4>Siljekær</t4> or <t5>Siliekier</t5>, and no such form has been found.
