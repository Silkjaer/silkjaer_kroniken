Chapter 9 · 1873—1963

The widow
from Årgab

There was no harbour at Årgab. There was a beach, a line of dunes and an open, restless sea.

The boats were launched into it by hand and hauled out of it again by hand. Årgab was one of the four old fishing hamlets on the narrow Holmsland Klit — together with Haurvig, Brunbjerg and Kokkedal, which are named together in a royal letter as early as 1553. In the dunes stood the fishermen’s huts — frame huts of turf, reed and marram — where the fishermen and the baiting girls stayed from April until Midsummer, with one double bed for the men and one for the women.

The local literature makes the women’s share of the work concrete: sorting out the fishing lines, mending, washing and drying them, baiting the hooks — ese — cleaning cod and boiling train oil from the livers, and on top of that cooking and keeping the hut clean. At Nymindegab a baiting girl’s day’s work is reckoned at around 2,400 hooks, four tubs of 600 — what one fisherman could set on a trip, and so there was one baiting girl to each fisherman. Fishing from the open beach, in other words, was not the men’s work alone; it rested on the women’s labour too. [S116]

It was here that the family lived into which the fisherman Thomas Nielsen Silkjær — Niels Thomsen’s eldest son, whom we have just met among the children from Økjær — would later marry. Kristiane Enevoldsen — in the sources also written Christiane — was born on the dunes on 27 March 1873, daughter of the farmer Simon Enevoldsen and Ane Marie Christensen in Haurvig.

On 1 April 1892, in Haurvig Church — whitewashed, neo-Romanesque and built four years before her own birth — she was married to Christen Olesen Enevoldsen, smallholder, fisherman and boat skipper at Årgab, born in the same place on 17 October 1871. They bore the same surname but belonged to two different Enevoldsen families on the dunes — in both cases the name came from a distant ancestor called Enevold.

As boat skipper Christen was responsible for the boat and its crew when it was launched through the surf and hauled up again. The boat was called “Forsøget” — the Attempt.

Four years after the wedding · 1 April 1896

Exactly four years after the wedding the boat was wrecked. She was called “Forsøget”, had been built the year before and was reckoned one of the soundest on the coast. She came in over the outer bar — and then the breaker rose behind her.

Four boats were out that morning — two from Årgab, two from Lyngvig — on the first trip of the year in the spring fishing. The other three came safely ashore. According to the account Ringkøbing Amts Dagblad carried in the days that followed, the sea lifted the stern so high that the boat went almost vertically down onto the bar and struck the sea bed.Ringkøbing Amts Dagblad has not been obtained. The course of events is known from Sigurd Enggaard Poulsen’s summary of the newspaper from 1977 and from Klinting’s account; the two agree with each other about the breaker, the stern and the blow against the sea bed. See caveat 41. [S134, S177] All caveats →

Five men were on board. Christen stood first on the list, because he was the skipper:

1Skipper Chr. Olesen Enevoldsen24 · married · smallholder at Årgab · b. Årgab 17 October 1871lost
2Peder Enevoldsen25 · married · smallholder at Årgab · b. Haurvig 8 August 1870lost
3Jens A. Larsen20 · bachelor · b. Klegod 3 September 1875lost
4N. Marius Christensen23 · bachelor · b. Sønder Bork 4 May 1872lost
5Fisherman Søren Andersen Jensen26 · married · smallholder at Årgab · b. Årgab 17 November 1869came ashore alive

The list hides a divide. Three of them — Christen, Peder Enevoldsen and Søren Andersen Jensen — were married smallholders at Årgab and owned the boat together. The other two were young farmhands from Klegod and Sønder Bork; they had no gear with them but were hired at a fixed wage for the trip. So the accident struck unequally. At Årgab both the breadwinners and the capital the boat represented were lost; the two young men left parents, not widows. [S177]

The fifth is the one to remember. Søren Andersen Jensen clung to an oar and drifted some six thousand alen south in the surf, followed by a crowd of fishermen on the shore who tried in vain to reach him. Then his strength failed too; he lost consciousness and let go of the oar. At that Enevold Enevoldsen leapt out after him with a line round his waist and caught hold of the unconscious man. He was taken for dead until the attempts to revive him worked — he came to life before the doctor from Ringkøbing arrived.

He was not the only one to catch hold of something. Peder Enevoldsen got a grip on a piece of the broken mast and drifted some 2,500 alen south with the current, while people followed along the beach. Then his strength gave out. He let go of the stump of mast and drowned.

The man with the line round his waist was Peder’s brother. That is the fact that ties the two fates together: the same family lost a son in the surf and pulled another woman’s husband out of it alive, a few thousand alen apart. Enevold Enevoldsen was later awarded the medal for saving life.The relationship and the medal are given by Klinting, whose informant for the account was, according to the text, Enevold Enevoldsen himself. He must not be confused with the boat skipper’s father, the fisheries inspector Enevold Jepsen Enevoldsen — they are two different men in two different Enevoldsen families. The medal has not been searched for in Statstidende. See caveat 41. [S177] All caveats →

The bodies came ashore the next day, Maundy Thursday, 2 April. Three of them had drifted up off Gødelen — Christen, Peder Enevoldsen and Jens A. Larsen. Christen lay on the strandlen in Bjerregaard that belonged to Peder Christian Nielsen, known on the dunes as Dahl. A strandlen was an allotted stretch of the coast where a particular farm had both the right and the duty over what the sea threw up — wreckage, timber and the drowned.The name rests on A.K. Sørensen’s family book, which writes “Peder Christian Dahl’s strandlen”, compared with Vragwiki’s list of strandlen on Holmsland Klit, which for Bjerregård gives “1885—89 P.C. Nielsen (Dahl)”. Dahl was the farm name, not the surname. Klinting instead names beach bailiff Jens Dahl, Bjerregaard, as the one who found the bodies. See caveat 42. [S56, S177, S180] All caveats →

Christen was buried from Haurvig Church on 6 April 1896 — Easter Monday — twenty-four years old, and Peder Enevoldsen followed in the same place. Jens A. Larsen was buried at Nørre Lyngvig. Niels Marius Christensen’s body only drifted ashore a couple of months later off Hovstrup and was buried in Sønder Bork. The boat itself came ashore smashed at the same spot. [S177]

Six weeks later, on 17 May 1896, Kristiane gave birth to a daughter: Kristine Oline. She never saw her father. She is a posthumous child — and we meet her again in chapters 12 and 13.

Whit Sunday · 24 May 1896

The stone came a month and a half after the wreck — and seven days after Kristine Oline was born. It was raised close to the road in Årgab village, on a fenced plot given by Knud Andersen, and it was paid for by the fishermen of Esbjerg.

Off the coast lay the gunboat “Grønsund”. Its commander, Captain Brædstrup, and First Lieutenant Reinhardt came ashore for the unveiling. At three o’clock they began with “Dejlig er Jorden”. Then Brædstrup handed over the stone on behalf of the Esbjerg fishermen to the fishermen of the dunes and asked the people of the dunes to protect it.

Some 500 people had come to the unveiling, surely one of the largest gatherings ever held down there on the dunes.
Dagens Nyheder · 28 May 1896 · after Ringkjøbing Avis [S178]

The harbour town gave the stone to the open beach. Esbjerg had a harbour, a mole and a fishermen’s association; Årgab had sand and muscle. It was the town with means that set up the memorial over those who had none.

Kristiane had given birth a week before. Whether she stood among the five hundred, the source does not say.The newspaper page has not been seen. Kristiane was then 23 and had two small sons: Enevold Jepsen, born on 13 October 1892 and named after his paternal grandfather, and Simon, born on 2 May 1894, named after his maternal grandfather.Both sons appear in the sources: the prison register of 1913 records Thomas as stepfather to “3 stepchildren”, and Enevold Jepsen, b. 13.10.1892, bears his paternal grandfather’s name. The birth entries have not been read in the original in Ny parish (Haurvig church district), births 1892—1894. [S31, S32, S44, S45] All caveats →

Ten years passed.

In 1906 she married again — the fisherman Thomas Nielsen Silkjær from Husby, almost nine years her junior. That marriage, and all that followed from it, are chapters 11 to 13.

In that marriage two families met who had each given the sea a man. Kristiane’s first husband drowned off Årgab in 1896. Thomas’ own grandfather, Thomas Jensen, drowned capsizing with a sea-boat off Husby in 1878.

A generation later the sons of that home sailed out on the same North Sea — during the war, with weapons for the resistance.

Kristiane Enevoldsen lived long. She died in Esbjerg on 31 July 1963, 90 years old.

The other widow

There was one more widow at Årgab after 1 April. Peder Enevoldsen’s wife was called Ane Marie, was born in 1868 and had two small children: Jensigne, born 1893, and Kresten. She stayed on in the little thatched house by the road, and she never married again.

The two women lost their husbands in the same boat, on the same afternoon, in the same little village. From there their lives went separate ways. Kristiane was widowed at twenty-three and married again at thirty-three and followed her new husband into everything chapters 11 to 13 are about. Ane Marie remained a widow for fifty-seven years. She died in 1953, eighty-five years old, and lies in Haurvig churchyard, where her husband was buried.

The sea took her son too. In March 1918 Kresten came home on Easter leave from military service in Copenhagen and went down to the harbour in Ringkøbing to find a boat across to Årgab. He sailed with two young men from the town in a south-westerly gale. They were warned, set sail all the same and capsized not far from the harbour. All three drowned and were buried at Easter.

Ane Marie does not belong to this family. She stands here because she is what Kristiane might also have become.This section is based on an account by Søren Rinder Kristensen, Årgab, with additions from Hardy Sørensen. Søren Rinder is a grandson of the survivor Søren Andersen Jensen; Hardy Sørensen is of Peder Enevoldsen’s family. It is family tradition written down in our own time, not archival record — neither Ane Marie’s dates, the children’s births nor the accident on Ringkøbing Fjord in 1918 have been checked in parish registers or newspapers.
