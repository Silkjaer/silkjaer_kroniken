Chapter 17 · 1945

Anne Kirstine
and the war in the North Sea

The story now leaves the heath and returns to the harbour in Esbjerg — to the sons of fishing skipper Thomas Nielsen Silkjær from Havnegade 179, whom we left as boys in chapter 11. This chapter is about the family’s cutter.

The first ANNE KIRSTINE had been built in Assens in 1897 of oak and pine, measured 34 gross register tons and came to Esbjerg in 1917. By 1945 she was an ageing workboat in a watched harbour: the Atlantic Wall ran like a line of concrete through the dunes, and the fishermen sailed with German eyes on them. The war cost 127 Danish fishermen their lives in the North Sea and the Skagerrak.

It was in this situation that the arms runs across the North Sea were to be started up again. The head of all airdrops and arms receptions in Jutland was known by the cover name Toldstrup; behind it stood the customs assistant Anton Jensen. He handed the work over to Lonsdale — cover name “Oscar Dahl” — and to the parish priest of Sdr. Onsild near Randers, Hans Kvist-Jensen, who went underground as “Jørgen Jensen” and many years later became Bishop of Roskilde. In December 1944 their group in Randers was broken up, and the two were sent to Esbjerg to re-establish the sea operations.

The priest had one thing no other envoy had: he was married to the daughter of Esbjerg’s former chairman of the fishermen’s association. And it was through his mother-in-law, Eline Christiansen, that he made contact with a fishing skipper who had the cutter ANNE KIRSTINE at his disposal. The skipper’s name was Niels Silkjær. He said yes — and found the next man himself: skipper Jens Frich of the cutter VEGA.

So the contact with the Esbjerg fishermen was not made through an organisation but through a woman who knew whom one could trust in the harbour. Once again in this story, the decisive connection runs through a kitchen and not an office.
The kinship

Niels and Ejlert were the sons of Thomas Nielsen Silkjær. Niels Silkær was born on 26 September 1910 at Aargab and baptised in Haurvig Church on 16 October; Ejlerth Marius Silkær was born on 29 January 1915 at Hjertinggade 13 in Esbjerg and baptised in Zion’s Church on 7 March.

With that the line is complete: the man who ran arms through Esbjerg harbour in March 1945 was the grandson of the smallholder from Økjær — and the great-grandson of the fisherman who drowned in 1878. Three generations, the same coast, the same sea.

The tax books say where the two men stood when it happened. Niels had a home of his own: Gormsgade 122, where the census of November 1940 lists four Silkjærs — Niels, Ane Kirstine, Jørgen Georg and Elisabeth. A father of two. His wife was Ane Kirstine, née Hansen, born on 10 September 1910 — sixteen days before him. They were married in Vor Frelsers Church on 21 December 1930, both twenty years old, six weeks after the census had found him living with his parents on Gl. Færgevej. She was an Esbjerg girl: born in Vor Frelsers parish, daughter of the brickworks labourer Jørgen Georg Hansen and his wife Ane Margrethe Kristensen Holm of Bag Teglværket 8, and herself a domestic help when they married. In August 1931 she gave birth to the son Jørgen Georg at Knudsgade 18 — the address the tax book gives for Niels the following year. He was given his maternal grandfather’s name, and at the baptism in Vor Frelsers Church the child’s paternal grandparents, fishing skipper Thomas Silkjær and Mrs Kristiane, stood as godparents. [S154] The daughter Elisabeth (1940—1997) arrived on 6 July 1940 at the Municipal Hospital; at her presentation in Zion’s Church in September both sets of grandparents stood as witnesses — Thomas and Kristiane from Havnegade 179 and the labourer Georg Hansen and his wife. She married a Jensen and died in Esbjerg in November 1997. [S155, S156] Ane Kirstine died in 1969; Niels was laid beside her seventeen years later. Ejlert still lived at Havnegade 179, but no longer as a son at home: he had married Dagmar Lodberg on 24 September 1939, and the census lists the couple as a household of their own in the house, with a single lodger. In the books he appeared as a share fisherman — a man on shares, not a skipper. He only became one in 1954, nine years later. So it was a fisherman on other men’s boats who travelled with the priest to Hvide Sande and Thyborøn.The tax books give only an initial, but the sequence is closed at both ends.   1. 6 JANUARY 1945
    The two boats were ready to sail. The meeting point had been agreed: a position on Outer Silver Pit, a fishing bank in the middle of the North Sea. Then the weather set in — truly foul winter weather with almost constant gales — and the boats stayed at home for ten weeks.
  2. 8 MARCH · THE WAIT PUT TO USE
    Kvist travelled with Ejlert Silkjær to Hvide Sande and got an immediate yes from the fishing skipper Jens Aae. But once Kvist had looked at the conditions, he gave up on the harbour: it could be watched from a German observation bunker, and the checks were unusually thorough. The two went on to Thyborøn, where the former Esbjerg fisherman Harald Iversen said yes and quickly found more skippers. Then they travelled back to Esbjerg.
  3. 16 MARCH 1945
    The weather was finally good enough. ANNE KIRSTINE and VEGA sailed.
    They reached the meeting point without trouble, and for the last stretch they were escorted by English aircraft that came in low over the cutters and waggled their wings.
  4. THE CARGO
    The goods were packed in watertight tin containers, placed in canvas bags. On VEGA the bags went down into the hold. ANNE KIRSTINE had a well — the water-filled compartment where the catch was normally kept alive — so her share had to be lowered into it. That went well enough, except for some containers of detonators: they would not sink and ended up hidden in a cupboard in the cabin. Besides the goods, the cutters were given paraffin, coffee, tea, whisky and cigars.
  5. EASTER SATURDAY · 31 MARCH
    Both cutters got safely through the harbour checks in Esbjerg and landed their fish.
  6. THE NIGHT AFTER
    The goods for the resistance were brought ashore a hundred metres from the German guards at the Halle restaurant. Nine mission fishermen carried the heavy load, while Kvist and the leader of the harbour company, Holger Nielsen, covered the operation with sub-machine guns. It went well. Everyone came home.
Esbjerg — Outer Silver Pit · 1945

The story should be made neither larger nor smaller than it is. Niels Silkjær said yes to running arms through a watched harbour in the last months of the war, brought Jens Frich in on VEGA, and his brother Ejlert helped the priest find more skippers — with the death penalty as the obvious consequence had they been discovered.

Preparations began at once for the next run, and in all six Esbjerg cutters had said yes. It never set out. So late in the war there was strong opposition to fishing for Germany, and the local resistance — which knew nothing of the arms transports — forbade the cutters to fish for anything but the home market. A few days later the Danish Freedom Council called on the Esbjerg cutters to sail to England. Esbjerg was thereby finished as a receiving port, and the work moved to Thyborøn. ANNE KIRSTINE’s run was the one that succeeded.

And then the proportions: eight cargoes in all reached Denmark by sea — an estimated 16 to 32 tons. Measured against what was dropped from the air, it is vanishingly little. The transports should therefore not be weighed by their military importance but by what they demanded: had a cargo been found, the fishermen would not even have had weapons to defend themselves. The skippers in Thyborøn were offered 10,000 kroner per cargo, and all three said no — they wanted only the normal price for the fish. Esbjerg’s own fleet register mentions it drily in the entry for E.94, between the tonnage and the list of owners: “Sailed arms to Esbjerg during the Second World War from English cutters.” A handful of words in a vessel register — the closest thing to official recognition the fishermen received.

From a marsh in Husby to the North Sea at war. The name had travelled far.
