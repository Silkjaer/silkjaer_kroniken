Appendix D · Sources and caveats

Sources and caveats

The main line Thomas Jensen → Niels Thomsen Silkjær → Ane Johanne is strongly supported by consistent, precise sources. Thomas Nielsen Silkjær as the Esbjerg skipper behind “T. N. Silkjær” is documented in a contemporary biographical source.

A number of matters are uncertain. Some are due to original entries not having been consulted, others to conflicting sources, difficult readings or later recollections. The most important caveats are marked with ? in the text where they arise; all of them are gathered here. The numbers are fixed references and do not change: 02, 03, 22 and 29 were withdrawn in the course of the work, and their numbers have not been reused.

The interpretation of the first element of the name — the willow marsh, from silje, an older word for willow — is possible, not established. The line of owners of cad. no. 13b is documented from Thomas Jensen’s deed in 1854/55 to the sale out of the family in 1924 and on through parts of the twentieth century; the year the house was built and the full line of occupants, by contrast, have not been established — the documentation is in Appendix A.

The youngest generations rest on the family’s own material and, out of consideration for the living, are kept to a minimum.

01
The grave after 17 November 1878
Thomas Jensen is named among the five dead in Dannevirke 13.11.1878 and Korsør Avis 14.11.1878 — who the three unnamed men were is clear from the newspapers. The newspaper, written on the day of the disaster, reports that none of the bodies had drifted ashore; the parish register’s collective note at the burial on 17 November singles out only Iver Chr. Olesen Kløve’s body as not having come ashore — by implication the other four, Thomas Jensen among them, came ashore in the intervening days. [S2, S25, S42, S43, S67]
02
Laura’s five children
The family file lists all five with dates of birth: Knud (1943—2019), Birte (1945), Ernst (1947), Edith (1949) and Leif (1956). The baptismal entries have not been read in the original, and the birthplaces of Birte, Ernst and Edith are not recorded. [S21, S40]
03
Boatman and Dannebrogsmand
C. P. Eisenreich’s official work on the North Jutland Lifeboat Service (1927) lists Niels Silkjær on page 314 with a portrait: appointed boatman at the Vedersø station on 1 January 1879, retired on grounds of age on 1 October 1916, “and shortly afterwards was decorated with the Dannebrogsmænd’s Badge of Honour”. The position, the length of service and the occasion of the badge are thus documented in a contemporary, official source, and the family tradition’s “bådsmand” corresponds to the ordinary rank of boatman — not boat foreman. Eisenreich’s “shortly afterwards” does not hold as a dating, however: the register of the Chapter of the Royal Orders records the award on 10 November 1915 — while he was still in service; cf. S110 and chapter 7. [S8, S15, S21, S41, S58, S110]
04
Kristiane’s sons
The family tells of two sons of Christen Olesen Enevoldsen: Enevold Jepsen, b. 13.10.1892, and Simon, b. 2.5.1894 — both mentioned in chapter 9. The birth entries in Nysogn (Haurvig church district), births 1892—1894, have not been read in the original. [S31, S32]
05
Johannes Silkjær, b. 1912
The family file lists Johannes Silkjær, b. 13.9.1912 at Oustrup, Øster Alling, as the eldest child of Jens Klaus and Bodil Marie, and the censuses have him as a farm boy in Vejlby in 1925 and 1930. The baptismal entry itself in the Øster Alling parish register has not been read. [S34, S40]
06
“FF Thomas Silkjær”
The Husby parish book (c. 1950) calls Thomas Jensen Thomas Silkjær in its list of Ane Marie’s grandparents. It is a retrospective source, and the contemporary entries of 1825—1878 call him Thomas Jensen. The earliest known record of the name attached to a person is the deed of 2 August 1876, where the buyer appears as Thomas Jensen Silkjær; the newspapers use the name in November 1878. In the censuses it first appears in 1890: there all five children appear as Nielsen Silkjær, while the parents are entered as Niels Thomsen and Ane Jensen. Whether it appears earlier in the Husby parish register has not been checked. The family sat on cad. no. 13b from 1855 — see caveat 19 — so the parish book’s name need not be a false memory but may reflect the home. [S27, S42, S43, S60, S64]
07
Ane Jensen’s parents
The parish book lists “MF Jens Claus, MM Stine” as Ane Marie’s maternal grandparents. Since Ane Marie’s own mother died when she was half a year old, the “Hustru Ane” (wife Ane) in the entry is her stepmother Ane Jensen — and Jens Claus and Stine are thus probably her parents. The family file gives them the full names Jens Claus Matzen, b. c. 1831 in Schleswig, and Ane Kirstine Hansdatter of Landtingmark, but those names do not appear in the parish book and have not been checked in the Ejsing parish register, births 1858. The local history booklet Husby Klit no. 8 records a schoolboy Jens Claus Mattzen in Husby: the parish board examined him in arithmetic on 15 April 1846 and granted his discharge from school, and the booklet ascribes the foreign-looking spelling of the surname to his having been born and baptised in Angeln in South Schleswig. The booklet does not itself connect the boy with Ane Jensen; the identification rests on the name, the parish and the age — discharge in 1846 fits a birth around 1831. If so, the family had moved: he went to school in Husby in 1846, his daughter was born at Landtingmark in Ejsing in 1858, and she came back to Husby at her marriage in 1881. [S27, S40, S169]
08
The Klit generations before 1814
The sequence Ib Jensen Klit and Jens Nielsen Klit (1710/c. 1720) → Ole Jensen Klit (1753) → Jens Olesen Klit (1785) rests on the family file’s own readings of the Husby parish register and probate records. The sequence is coherent and rooted in one place, but the oldest entries are summary, and the individual generations have not been checked in the original. That also applies to the title of beach bailiff, which stands in the family file without reference to a letter of appointment. [S40]
09
The neighbours’ daughter, December 1912
The prison register does not name the neighbours’ daughter with whom Thomas Nielsen Silkjær left home. The identification as Nielsine Nielsen rests on the court records of 1913, where he himself names her — “en ugift Pige fra Aargab, Nielsine Nielsen” (an unmarried girl from Aargab, Nielsine Nielsen) — taken together with the parish register entry at Else Margrethe’s birth the same year, where he appears as the declared father. The two sources are independent of each other and point to the same woman. On her and the child otherwise: see caveat 32. [S44, S45, S119, S124]
10
Readings in the prison register, 1913
The “Gl.” in Gl. Færgevej 7 is worn, but the census of 1930 lists the household on the same street; by 1916 the family had moved to Hjertinggade 13, so that census can neither confirm nor refute the reading. The number field “Er gift, har __ Børn” (Is married, has __ children) has been written over, and the figure is doubtful — the note beneath, “1 Blodskamsbarn og 3 Stedbørn” (1 child of incest and 3 stepchildren), is by contrast clear and agrees with the three children Kristiane had with Christen Olesen Enevoldsen. The height 63¾ is entered without a unit; read as Danish inches it corresponds to just over 167 cm, but the unit is not on the form. The words of the teachers’ assessments, kvindekær (fond of women) and ordentlig (orderly), are read from the context. The end of the sentence is entered as 27 April 1914. The punishment register of Ringkjøbing Gaol records the sentence as begun on 27 April 1913 at 8 a.m.; the date therefore does not cover custody on remand, but the first day of the sentence served. The recorded end thus corresponds to exactly one year from the start of the sentence, not to the judgment’s eighteen months — and it fits exactly with § 13 of the Penal Code: when served in solitary confinement the term was shortened by a third, so eighteen months became twelve. The calculation has been applied to the dates in the prison register and the punishment register; that the sentence was served in a cell is not stated on the form itself, but it was the law’s main rule for prisoners in the house of correction. The date rests on one source, not two. [S44, S45, S46, S76, S87, S117]
11
The two godparents from 1896
Two of Arne’s four godparents bear names from the crew list of “Forsøget”. The smallholder Enevold Jepsen Enevoldsen, b. 11.1.1848, is Kristine Oline’s paternal grandfather — the “Svigerfader” (father-in-law) whom the prison register says Thomas came to work for, and whose property at Aargab they took over. A.K. Sørensen’s Enevoldsen family book gives Enevold Jeppesen Enevoldsen and Ane Iversen as the parents of the drowned boat skipper; it dates the father’s birth, however, to 10.1.1848 against the family tree’s 11.1. He appears four times around the family: godparent at Kristen’s baptism in 1904, witness at the wedding in 1906, summoned to court as Kristine Oline’s natural guardian in March 1913, and godparent at Arne’s baptism that summer. The appearance as guardian is unambiguously his: the police record book lists him as a former fishery warden, and the title fits only the grandfather. The name is also borne by Kristine Oline’s elder brother, b. 13.10.1892. At Kristine Oline’s wedding in 1916 “Fisker Enevold J. Enevoldsen, Esbjerg” (fisherman Enevold J. Enevoldsen, Esbjerg) stands as witness — it may be the grandfather or the elder brother, so the fifth appearance is ambiguous. The place of residence points to the elder brother: the grandfather and Ane Iversen formed a household of two in Nysogn at the census of 1 February 1916, seven months before the wedding, and he is not at Helgolandsgade 14 in 1921 either. He did, however, die at that very address in 1923, 75 years old — having moved there in his last couple of years — so the address cannot settle the question of the witness. That it was the same Enevold Enevoldsen who pulled the survivor ashore in 1896 cannot be documented — several men of that name lived at Aargab at the same time. The smallholder Søren Andersen Jensen is just as firm in his own person: name, place and title are the same in 1896 and 1913, and he is registered at Årgab from his birth on 17.11.1869 to his death on 29.12.1937. [S31, S32, S40, S47, S48, S55, S56, S70, S71, S75, S77, S78, S80, S119]
12
What became of Arne
Arne Enevoldsen married Annagrethe Thomsen on 11.5.1940 — the marriage has been read in the Zion parish register, cf. S85 — and appears in the census of 5.11.1940 on Rolfsgade in Esbjerg; the census comes from the family file and has not been read in the original. The name is written Annagrethe in the parish register, Annegrethe in the census and in the archive’s captions. Esbjerg City Archives holds three Paul Bølling negatives from 1943—45 ordered by the Enevoldsen family, Rolfsgade 127, with the text “revisor Arne Enevoldsen (f. 1913) og hustruen Annegrethe (f. 1916)” (accountant Arne Enevoldsen, b. 1913, and his wife Annegrethe, b. 1916) and the daughter, born in May 1944. The identification rests on year of birth, address and the wife’s name, all of which agree. The archive itself is guarded about who is seen in the two birth pictures. Arne’s death on 27 December 1998 in Vejle is documented by the death notice — see caveat 25. [S40, S57, S147]
13
The wreck and the revival
That the wreck of 1 April 1896 brought the Inner Mission revival its breakthrough on the southern dunes rests first and foremost on Chr. Sørensen’s recollection in Tidsskrift for Redningsvæsen 1944 — written down forty-eight years afterwards, with a mistaken number of dead (“et Par Fiskere”, a couple of fishermen; there were four) and with Pastor Nedergaard’s words at the funeral quoted from memory. The revival did not begin with the disaster: there was Inner Mission activity on the dunes from around 1890. The priest in the quotation can be named — Peder Christensen Nedergaard (1868—1945) was resident curate on Holmsland and lived so close that his son Paul was born at Årgab in 1895 — but that it was he who conducted the funeral on 6.4.1896 rests on Sørensen’s recollection alone; the Nysogn parish register, deaths 1896, has not been read. No available material on the mission houses mentions Kristiane Enevoldsen or the name Silkjær. [S49, S50, S51, S52]
14
Peder Enevoldsen
Among the dead from “Forsøget” is Peder Enevoldsen, b. in Haurvig 8.8.1870 — almost the same age as the boat skipper and with the same surname, which is easily read as brothers. A.K. Sørensen’s Enevoldsen family book gives them different fathers: the boat skipper Christen Olesen Enevoldsen was the son of Enevold Jeppesen Enevoldsen and Ane Iversen, while Peder Enevoldsen was the son of the smallholder Christen Nielsen Enevoldsen and Karen Marie Nielsen. So they were probably not brothers. Klinting’s account points the same way: it gives Peder Enevoldsen’s brother as Enevold Enevoldsen, that is, a different man from the boat skipper’s father Enevold Jepsen Enevoldsen. The family book is a secondary source; Nysogn (Haurvig church district), births 1870—1871, has not been read. [S40, S48, S56, S177]
15
“Chr. Sørensen”, 1944
The journal names the man only by his initial. Birthday, age, occupation and residence all coincide with Christen Olesen Sørensen, b. at Aargab 6.5.1849, fisherman and beach bailiff in the same place, d. in Esbjerg 25.3.1948 and married for the second time in 1892 to Ane Cathrine Jørgensen — which agrees with his “ogsaa min Kone og jeg” (my wife and I too). The identification is probable, but not verified in the text of the interview itself. He is not the same man as the boat skipper Christen Olesen Enevoldsen. [S49, S53]
16
The mission houses and the name Silkjær
Getsemane (Haurvig 1899), Thabor (Holmsland 1899) and Ebenezer (Sønder Lyngvig 1910) are all documented in available accounts, but in none of the material does the name Silkjær or Kristiane Enevoldsen appear. The connection between the family and the revival thus remains indirect — it runs through the wreck of 1.4.1896, not through the mission houses. The houses’ own minute books, building committee lists and anniversary publications have not been examined, so a name may still turn up there. Thyge Andersen Enevoldsen, who paid for Ebenezer, cannot be linked to Kristiane’s branch beyond the shared surname. [S50, S51, S55]
17
The five that are missing
Eisenreich writes that Niels Silkjær took part 7 times with the lifeboat and 8 times with the rocket apparatus in the rescue of 195 shipwrecked people. If his period of service, 1.1.1879—1.10.1916, is laid over the station’s own list of successful rescue operations, the result is exactly fifteen strandings — the figure fits. But the distribution does not: the list gives six of them as boat operations and nine with rocket. At the “Speed” in 1885, according to the book’s own account, the lifeboat was worked for two hours before they gave up and saved the crew with the rocket, and that one difference can probably be explained there. The main figure is harder: the fifteen strandings add up to 200 rescued, not 195. Either he was not counted in one of the operations, or there is an error of counting in the book. The lifeboat service’s own crew lists and reports have not been examined. [S58]
18
When the crew photograph was taken
The note behind the photograph says “ca. 1910-20”, and another hand has written 1915. Eisenreich’s information narrows it from both sides: Poul Pedersen Bank, who appears as superintendent on the note, only became so on 1 July 1908, and Niels Silkjær retired on 1 October 1916 — so the picture lies between those two dates. But number ten on the note, Peder Sand Pedersen, retired according to the book as early as 1 October 1910. Either the picture is older than 1915, or a man who had left is in it, or there are two different men of the same name. The year 1915 is given after the archive’s own inscription; the discrepancy has not been resolved. [S37, S58]
19
Cad. no. 13b, Silkjær and Økjær
The censuses of 1890, 1901 and 1911 list the household of Niels Thomsen — later Niels Thomsen Silkjær — and Ane Jensen on cad. no. 13b in Øe, Husby, the number on which Silkjær lies today; the census of 1880 lists Niels Thomsen and his first wife, Ane Johanne Kristensen, in Øe without a cadastral number. The land register and the deed and mortgage books record the ownership: Thomas Jensen received the deed to the parcel in 1854/55, Niels Thomsen obtained title by allotment in January 1879, and the property was sold out of the family in 1924. The census of 1 February 1855 falls between the signing of the deed (18 December 1854) and its registration (4 April 1855) and already lists Thomas Jensen as head of household; his wife appears as Anne Marie Jensen, 28, born in Vedersø parish — age and parish of birth correspond to Anne Marie Jansdatter. The parish registers use Økjær as the place of residence in six original entries from 1855 to 1896, in the forms Øekjær and Økjær; the designation covered the area and several households, not one building, and so does not contradict the ownership of 13b. Where the boundary of the area ran is not known. The books have been read in photographic copies; hartkorn figures, sums and a few names have not been checked in the original. The registration date of the deed of 19 June 1851 is an uncertain reading. The seller’s byname in the 1855 deed is difficult to decipher; the family’s records identify him as Niels Jensen Poulsgaard (1802—1862), born in Øe, which fits the census’s age and stepdaughter — the deed’s byname is an uncertain reading, and the identification therefore rests on the records. The 1851 deed’s list “med dens Tilliggende samt Avl og Afgrøde” (with its appurtenances, crops and harvest) — without buildings — makes it likely that the house came into being in 1851—54, but does not prove it; that very passage is moreover an uncertain reading. The wording “min abalienerede Gaards Jorder” (the lands of my alienated farm) may mean that Jens Thomsen had already disposed of the farm itself by June 1851 — this has not been clarified in the sources consulted. And the registration of the 1847 deed has been read both as 29 March and as 29 October 1848; the date cannot be settled from the photographic copy. [S40, S60, S61, S62, S63, S64]
20
The levy roll 1900—1910
Thomas Nielsen Silkjær appears in the levy roll for Husby (levy district 28 in the 5th conscription area, serial no. 2) without any intervening removal references until May 1906 — if a conscript moved parish, it had to be reported. The rule was not followed consistently, however: the place of service in Vedersø in 1901 has left no reference, so the roll’s silence cannot be used to rule out stays outside Husby — at Årgab included — before the marriage. The endorsements have been read in photographic copy: “28/9 03. Søfbog.” (seaman’s book), “U. A. 1905” and “E.K.” — actual military service — and his arrival in Esbjerg (levy district 33a, letter D, no. 160) on 21 May 1906 on a removal certificate of 12 May. In 1910 (letter H) he is transferred to levy district 36, which according to the key to the levy rolls is Holmsland Klit, with a recall to the Navy on 23—30 October 1910. The prison register’s “boet 2 Aar i Esbjerg” (lived 2 years in Esbjerg) before taking over the property at Aargab does not square with the roll’s 1910; the roll is the dated source. The census of 1 February 1901 lists him as a farmhand with Peder Sand Pedersen on cad. no. 67 at Klit By, Vedersø parish — written “Tomas Nielsen Silkær”, unmarried, born on 10 January 1882 in Husby; date and place of birth agree with the roll. [S44, S45, S72, S74]
21
Arne’s two forms
The census of 1916 lists Arne as “Arne Silkjær” under Children, with no distinction from the household’s other children; the census of 1930 lists him as “Arne Enevoldsen”, foster child. Both can be defended: his legal name was Enevoldsen, so the 1930 form is the precise one — but the 1916 form may just as well reflect everyday speech about a two-year-old in the house. Why the two censuses list him differently cannot be determined; both readings are given. [S46, S76]
22
Kristine Oline after 1916
The marriage on 19 September 1916, the households in 1921, 1925 and 1930 and the death on 18 May 1940 are documented: Helgolandsgade 14 in 1921 and 1925, Cort Adelersgade 15b in November 1930, Frodesgade 149 at the death certificate. Column 7 of the certificate gives the principal illness as c. uteri with cachexia as a complication, column 8 the immediate cause of death as heart failure, paresis cordis; the Register of Deaths’ transcript gives only column 8, and the chronicle follows the certificate. How long she had been ill the certificate does not say — cachexia presupposes a longer course, but the duration is an inference and not information. The certificate’s own fields for the parish register entry and the committal are blank, but the entry exists: the Zion parish register of deaths records the burial on 22 May 1940 in Esbjerg New Cemetery by Pastor Birke — the cemetery now called Gormsgade. The number and location of the grave have not been obtained, and the cemetery’s own registers have not been examined — that her stepfather and his father lie in the same cemetery therefore says nothing about how close the graves are. Her daughter Gerda’s birth on 23 February 1917 appears in the censuses of 1921, 1925 and 1940; the parish register entry has not been read. Her first trace in Esbjerg is the census of 1 February 1916: Nørregade 44, Vor Frelsers parish, entered as an unmarried servant girl with the occupation domestic help in the household of Anders Hansen Larsen and Anne Marie Larsen, their children and a lodger. Taken together with the 1921 census’s year of arrival, 1916, from Holmsland, she must have moved in the very first weeks of the year — or else the year of arrival has been rounded; the 1921 column is a later self-declaration and does not carry precision to the week. Where on Holmsland she was between 1913 and the move remains open — and the censuses cannot settle it: the only census between 1911 and 1921 is that of 1 February 1916, when she was already in Esbjerg. [S75, S77, S78, S79, S81, S82, S83, S84, S133]
23
The censuses’ years of birth
In the household at Hjertinggade 13 in 1916, the years of birth of three of the seven people differ by one year from the parish registers’, while day and month agree. The censuses’ years of birth therefore cannot carry weight on their own; the parish register is the dating source. [S76]
24
The causes of death, 1863 and 1869
The register of deaths in Husby has a column for the cause of death. For Anne Marie Jansdatter and Kjerstine Thomsen in 1863 it is blank, and it is blank in several of the parish’s other entries the same year — the silence is thus the year’s registration practice and not an omission concerning this house. In 1869 the column is in use: “død af Halssyge” (died of throat sickness) stands beside a two-year-old, a four-and-a-half-year-old, two adults of thirty and the entry immediately before Karen Marie Thomsen’s. Beside her own name there is nothing. Pastor Hornbech
The travelling preacher to whom Kristine Oline wrote is entered in the police record book as “Pastor Hornbeck fra Randers, der som Rejsepræst for Indre Mission flere Gange har besøgt Klitten i de senere Aar” (Pastor Hornbeck of Randers, who as travelling preacher for the Inner Mission has several times visited the dunes in recent years). He is identified with high probability as Carl Marius Hornbech (1871—1934): matriculated from Randers Latin School in 1890, cand.theol. 1896, missionary in India 1902—09 — where his first wife Olga died in 1908 — then travelling preacher from 1909, living in Randers, and from 1915 parish priest at St Michael’s Church in Fredericia, where he died in 1934. That a Pastor Hornbech lived in Randers in exactly the years of the case is independently confirmed by the town’s school programmes for 1912—14, which list a child with the father given as “Hornbech (Præst)”. One discrepancy has since been cleared up: the police record book calls him travelling preacher for the Inner Mission, but the police report in the criminal file — written three weeks earlier, from the family’s own information — lists him as “en Pastor Hornbæk, der bor i Randers, men som er Rejsepræst for dansk Missionsselskab” (a Pastor Hornbæk who lives in Randers but is travelling preacher for the Danish Missionary Society). That is precisely Carl Marius Hornbech’s society; the record book’s “Inner Mission” must then be counted as the clerk’s imprecision, and the identification is strengthened accordingly. The report also confirms the letter: the priest had told Anders Hejde of Bjerregaard that he had “modtaget Brev fra den besvangrede om Forholdet” (received a letter from the pregnant girl about the matter), and that she had written to him because he had held a service on the dunes, “og at hun ikke havde syntes, at hun kunde betro sig til nogen her hjemme” (and that she had not felt she could confide in anyone here at home). The name is spelt three ways in the material: the police record book’s “Hornbeck”, the travelling police court book’s and the report’s “Hornbæk” and the man’s own form “Hornbech”. The chronicle uses Hornbech in its own text, while quotations and transcriptions keep the sources’ spellings — that also applies to the label “the Hornbeck passage”, which names the record book’s own wording. No source otherwise connects him directly with the case. His letter to her has not been found, and it is not known whether it was ever handed in to the authorities; the scene with the letter stands as Kristiane told it in court on 7 March. The pardon case and the date of the resolution
The chain in the pardon case is documented in the archives of the county and the prison: the defence counsel, solicitor Kamp, submitted the petition; the judge recommended it in a statement of 7 July 1913; the county forwarded it on 8 July to the Ministry of Justice through the Director of Prisons “idet bem., at Amtet kan henholde sig til den af Pol. afgivne Erklæring” (noting that the county can refer to the statement given by the police); the ministry refused on 17 July, and the refusal was sent on to the institution and entered in its journal. S117 Vridsløselille as an institution in 1913: the house of correction on Vridsløselille Mark (from 1933 Vridsløselille State Prison) was organised on the Philadelphia system — sentences served in solitary confinement day and night, a mask outside the cell, a ban on conversation and stalls in church and school; compulsory masks were only made voluntary in 1924, and the principle of isolation remained central until the Penal Code of 1930, in force in 1933. The Penal Code of 1866 at the same time laid down a reduction of the term for sentences served in solitary confinement — isolation was in itself considered an aggravating element — so that the actual time served by prisoners in the house of correction lay between 6 months and 3½ years, against sentences of 8 months to 6 years; the reduction was maintained until 1933. § 13 of the Act set the reduction at one third for the first three years; eighteen months of hard labour in the house of correction thereby became twelve, which corresponds exactly to the prison register’s date of release (27 April 1914, one year after the start of the sentence). The conversion has been checked against the text of the law; that the sentence was in fact served in solitary confinement is the law’s main rule and an inference from the date, not information in the prison register.
